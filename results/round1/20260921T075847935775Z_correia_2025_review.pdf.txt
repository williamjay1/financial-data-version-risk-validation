This paper reviews the extensive literature on the predictive power of accounting information
for bankruptcy . Prior research demonstrates that financial statement information effectively
predicts bankruptcy out-of-sample, both independently and in combination with market
data. I discuss several attributes of accounting information that may enhance or impair its
utility in bankruptcy prediction. Using a comprehensive dataset of bankruptcies from 1980
to 2023, I analyse how the role of accounting information in credit risk assessment has
evolved over the past four decades. My findings reveal that the predictive power of models
based solely on accounting information has remained stable over the most recent decades,
whereas the predictive power of equity market information has shown a modest increase.
Notably , the performance of accounting and market-based models does not always align. In
periods of declining market information efficacy , accounting information often remains
robust, mitigating the impact on combined models. Conversely , market information
frequently offsets reductions in the predictive power of accounting data, underscoring the
complementary strengths of these information sources.
Keywords: bankruptcy prediction; default; credit risk
1. Introd uction
Corporate failures impose significant costs on a broad spectrum of stakeholders, including inves-
tors, creditors, employees, auditors, regulators, and society at large. These failures often trigger
contagion ef fects, resulting in far-r eaching consequences at both industry and macroeconomic
levels. Corporate failure, broadly defined, occurs when a company fails to meet its responsibil-
ities to stakeholders, such as shareholders, employees, customers, and the broader community .
Such failures can stem from various causes, including macroeconomic shocks, technological dis-
ruptions, or agency problems. Prominent examples like W irecard and Carillion underscore the
profound economic and social costs of corporate failure, which often culminates in financial

Extensive research has examined the role of accounting information in forecasting financial
distress. A foundational contribution to this field is Beaver (1966), which identifies six categories
of financial statement ratios with significant predictive ability for bankruptcy . Building on
Beaver ’ s seminal work, subsequent studies assess the predictive power of accounting infor-
mation both in isolation and in combination with other sources, such as equity , credit, and
option data. These studies consistently highlight the incremental value of accounting information
in predicting financial distress, even when market data are available. However , as Correia,
Richardson, and T una (2012) and Correia, Richardson and Kang (2018) note, the role of account-
ing information in this context is not always fully appreciated by credit market investors.
Over the years, empirical methods for predicting financial distress have grown increasingly
sophisticated, spurred by advancements in machine learning techniques, the availability of non-
traditional data sources, and increased computing power . Despite these advancements, Beaver ’ s
core finding – that accounting information has predictive power for bankruptcy – has remained
remarkably robust. This predictive ability has persisted despite major changes in accounting
standards and a shift from conservative to fair value accounting practices, changes that some
feared would diminish the reliability of accounting information and its relevance for creditors.
Naturally , isolating the ef fects of changes in accounting standards from concurrent macroeco-
nomic shifts or other regulatory interventions (e.g. Sarbanes Oxley , Dodd-Frank Act) presents
a significant challenge.
Much of the earlier literature on bankruptcy prediction developed without an explicit theor-
etical framework. The Merton (1974) model of credit spreads, however , provides a valuable
foundation for understanding the role of accounting information in this context. In the Merton
model, the key construct is the market value of the firm’ s assets and its dynamic evolution
over time. Default occurs when the market value of the firm’ s assets falls below a critical
threshold, referred to as the default barrier . This framework employs standard option-pricing
techniques to model asset value evolution, which depends on the initial asset value, asset vola-
tility , and drift. The probability of default increases as the distance between the firm’ s asset value
and its default barrier narrows, particularly when asset volatility is high.
W ithin the Merton (1974) framework, accounting information becomes critical for bank-
ruptcy prediction by providing insights into three essential components: the value of the firm’ s
assets (Duffie and Lando, 2001), the dynamics of asset evolution – including volatility and
drift terms (Correia et al. 2018) – and the default barrier , representing the firm’ s debt obligations
(Giesecke 2006). This framework offers a structured approach to integrating accounting data into
bankruptcy risk evaluation. It also highlights potential limitations of the accounting system,
serving as a basis to evaluate how changes in accounting standards and disclosure regulation
may impact the predictive power of accounting information for future defaults.
In this paper , I begin by laying out the Merton (1974) framework, as a starting point to under-
standing the potential role of accounting information in default and bankruptcy prediction. I then
review the accounting and finance literatures on the role of financial ratios in bankruptcy prediction,
highlighting some of their key findings. In doing so, I also touch on the findings of a more recent
literature that investigates the extent to which accounting information is reflected in credit spreads.
I then re-examine the incremental predictive value of accounting information for bankruptcy
using a long time-series of data that extends to 2023. I show that accounting information has

sources.

on the potential limitations of financial statement data for default and bankruptcy prediction, as
well as on the characteristics of accounting information that render it more (or less) useful for
bankruptcy prediction.
Finally , I explore outstanding questions and suggest several avenues for future research.
2. A theoretical framework
Much of the bankruptcy prediction literature developed without a formal theoretical framework.
Nonetheless, some of the earlier studies reference the inventory-theoretic approach proposed by
Baumol (1952). In Baumol’ s model, a company is conceptualised as a reservoir of liquid assets,
replenished by inflows such as cash generated from operations and depleted by outflows like debt
obligations and expenses. This reservoir functions as a buf fer against fluctuations in cash flows,
with financial distress modelled as the probability that the reservoir becomes empty , leaving the
company unable to meet its obligations.
According to this framework, the likelihood of financial distress increases when the size of
the reservoir , representing the firm’ s liquid assets, is insufficient. Similarly , financial distress is
more likely when expected inflows, such as net cash flows from operations, are lower , or when
expected outflows, such as debt repayment obligations, are higher . Additionally , greater variabil-
ity in cash inflows, reflecting heightened volatility in operational cash flows, further raises the
risk of the reservoir being depleted. This simple yet intuitive model underscores the importance
of liquid assets, the stability of cash flows, and the balance between inflows and outflows (all of
which can be approximated by financial statement variables) in assessing a firm’ s vulnerability to
financial distress and bankruptcy .
Some of the more recent studies on default prediction build on the seminal work of Merton
(1974). This framework is particularly helpful when thinking about the properties of account-
ing information that that might render it more useful in predicting future firm failure.
In the Merton model, the central construct is the firm’ s asset value and its evolution over time.

Although the Merton model is simplistic, its framework has been extended in the literature to
account for complexities such as differe nt types of debt, stochastic interest rates, bankruptcy
costs, and tax effects (e.g. Black and Cox 1976, Geske 1977, Longstaf f and Schwartz 1995,
Leland and T oft 1996).
While neither the value of the firm’ s assets nor its volatility are directly observable, they can
be inferred under the model’ s assumptions by iteratively solving a system of nonlinear equations
based on equity value, equity volatility , and other variables. This exercise presumes that the strict
assumptions of the Merton model are valid. Alternatively , one can use the Merton model as a
framework to guide the choice of bankruptcy predictors and the way in which they are combined.
This second approach seems to dominate the first (please refer to Section 3.2 for a discussion),
which suggests that the Merton model does not fully capture reality , in part due to its stringent
assumptions.
Despite its limitations, the Merton model provides a valuable framework for understanding
the of accounting information (both financial ratios and accounting disclosures more broadly) in
default prediction. It highlights that accounting information can have incremental predictive
power for bankruptcy when it provides additional insights into a firm’ s initial asset values,
their evolution – i.e. asset volatility and asset return drift – and default barriers (i.e. leverage).
This framework is also instrumental in identifying scenarios where the accounting system may
generate less reliable measures of these key variables, reducing the relevance of accounting
information for assessing credit risk. Conversely , it helps pinpoint situations where accounting
information is likely to be particularly valuable in evaluating financial health. As such, this
offers important insights for standard setters, whose objective is to provide relevant and reliable
information to stakeholders, including data with strong predictive value for creditors’ decision-
making.

3. The r ole of accounting information in default prediction
3.1. Reduced form models
Bankruptcy prediction models initially developed with little or no theoretical guidance, emerging
from practical needs before being formalised in academic literature. The origins of credit analysis
for estimating default and bankruptcy risk can be traced back to the late nineteenth century (Kur -
unmäki and Miller 2013). During this period, lar ge manufacturers and wholesalers began
employing ‘credit men’ to assess the creditworthiness of existing and prospective clients.
These professionals were instrumental in the development of accounting standards, advocating
for uniform financial statements and contributing to the widespread adoption of ratio analysis. By
the late 1890s, banks had also begun to rely heavily on financial ratios for credit decision-
making.
A significant milestone in the development of credit analysis occurred in 1919 when Alex-
ander W all, at the request of the Federal Reserve Board, conducted a study on ‘credit baro-
metrics.’ His work, followed by others such as W all and Duning (1928) and Fitzpatrick
(1932) proposed a list of accounting ratios believed to be strong predictors of credit quality .
These early efforts often involved univariate analyses or arbitrary weighting of ratios, setting
the stage for more systematic approaches.
Beaver (1966) expands on these early effo rts by systematically comparing the levels and
changes of 30 financial ratios for bankrupt and non-bankrupt firms in the years preceding bank-
ruptcy . He groups these ratios into six categories: (i) cash flow ratios; (ii) net income ratios;
(iii) debt-to-total-asset ratios; (iv) liquid-asset-to-total-asset ratios; (v) liquid-asset-to-current-debt
ratios; and (vi) turnover ratios. From each group, he selects one representative ratio for more detailed
analysis: cash flow to total debt, income to total assets, total debt to total assets, working capital to
total assets, current assets to current liabilities, and a turnover ratio. His findings highlight significant
differences in both the levels and changes of these ratios between bankrupt and non-bankrupt firms
in the years leading up to bankruptcy . These results suggest that each ratio has incremental expla-
natory power for bankruptcy prediction, though this predictive power varies across ratios. Beaver ’ s
analysis emphasises the importance of combining multiple financial statement variables to assess the
predictive power of accounting information and lays the groundwork for a more systematic
approach to using financial ratios in predicting financial distress and bankruptcy .
Another seminal early study in bankruptcy prediction is Altman (1968). Altman (1968)
examines five variables using a multiple discriminant analysis: a liquidity measure (working
capital to total assets), a measure of cumulative profitability (retained earnings to total assets),
a profitability measure (earnings before interest and taxes to total assets), a leverage ratio
(market value of equity to total liability), and a total asset turnover ratio (sales to total assets).
Based on this analysis, Altman proposes the Z-Score, a score still broadly used both in the
academic literature and in practice.

already explored by Beaver (1966) and Altman (1968).

Shumway (2001) shows that a hazard model off ers both theoretical and empirical advantages
over static models, such as the conditional logit model employed by Ohlson (1980). The hazard
model accounts for period at risk and accommodates time-varying covariates, enabling it to
leverage all available information to estimate bankruptcy probabilities at each point in time.
This dynamic approach mitigates the selection biases inherent in static models, providing
more accurate and robust predictions. In addition to a novel estimation approach, Shumway pro-
poses a model that combines a series of accounting and market variables, including return on
assets, accounting leverage, market size, past stock returns, and the idiosyncratic volatility of
stock returns. Importantly his findings confirm that accounting information retains predictive
power when combined with equity market data, despite the latter potentially reflecting a
broader mix of information, including insights derived from the accounting variables themselves.
Along a similar line, Beaver , McNichols and Rhie (2005) compare the predictive ability of
three models: (i) an accounting model; (ii) a market model; and a (iii) combined model that inte-
grates the predictors in the accounting and market models.

hensive financial analysis, utilising more granular financial statement information alongside
other financial and non-financial data, and applying more sophisticated statistical techniques.

3.2. Structural models
Numerous studies explore the predictive power of distance-to-default measures derived from the
Merton (1974) structural framework. Hillegeist et al. (2004), for instance, find that a distance-to-
default measure outperforms modified versions of both the O-Score and Z-Score in predicting
default. Bharath and Shumway (2008) compares compare the predictive ability of an implied dis-
tance-to-default metric with a reduced-form model that incorporates key inputs from the Merton
framework. Their findings suggest that reduced-form models often outperform the Merton
approach, primarily due to the latter ’ s reliance on stringent assumptions that do not fully
capture reality and limit its applicability . By combining the Merton-implied distance-to-
default metric with additional predictors, they achieve enhanced predictive accuracy . Similarly ,
Campbell et al. (2008) show that reduced-form econometric approaches, which allow for more
flexible treatment of volatility , leverage, and other variables, outperform structural approaches in
predicting corporate failure.
While these findings indicate that a fully structural approach – simultaneously estimating an
implied value and volatility of assets – does not maximise out-of-sample predictive power ,
several studies build on the Merton (1974) model to develop theoretically grounded bankruptcy
predictors based on financial statement data. For instance, Correia et al. (2018) focus on asset
volatility and investigate whether fundamental risk measures can be used to improve on existing
models.

3.3. Expanding the use of financial and non-financial information
Recent studies have sought to expand the set of bankruptcy predictors, leveraging advance-
ments in data availability and computational power . These studies often develop theoretically
motivated predictors, which they then test empirically . For instance, Beaver et al. (2019,
2024) investigate the role of granular within-group financial information. They find that a
bankruptcy prediction model based on consolidated group financial statements can be signifi-
cantly enhanced by incorporating bankruptcy probability data for individual entities within the
group. These findings suggest that intra-group exposures – often netted out during consolida-
tion – contain valuable credit-relevant information that contributes to more accurate predic-
tions of financial distress.
Other studies have explored the use of narrative disclosures as predictors of bankruptcy . For
instance, Mayew , Sethuraman, and V enkatachalam (2015) find that management’ s opinion on
going concern, as expressed in the Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) section of
the annual report, along with the tone of this section, has predictive power for future bankruptcy
(up to three years ahead). This predictive power is incremental to both financial statement and
equity market data. Similarly , Donovan et al. (2021) show that qualitative disclosures in the
10-K report and conference calls also provide predictive insights into future bankruptcy ,
adding value beyond a set of financial statement variables. Auditor going concern opinions
have also been shown to offer valuable information (Gutierrez et al. 2020), as they encapsulate
a complex set of conditions that are often beyond the reach of traditional default predictors.
Notably , even with the incorporation of these additional predictors, the incremental predictive
power of traditional accounting ratios remains significant.
Jones (2017) examines a broad set of bankruptcy predictors, including ownership concen-
tration, the percentage of institutional and insider ownership, and CEO compensation. The
study finds that several of these governance-related variables rank among the most powerful pre-
dictors of bankruptcy , surpassing traditional accounting ratios in predictive strength. This finding
is consistent with earlier literature documenting a role of corporate governance in bankruptcy
prediction (e.g. Daily and Dalton 1994). In contrast, analyst forecasts and recommendations con-
tribute relatively little to prediction accuracy , which is consistent with prior findings that analysts
often respond with a delay to significant increases in default risk (Jones and Johnstone 2012,
Precourt and Oppenheimer 2017).
Finally , a few studies investigate the influence of personal characteristics of executives and
rank-and-file employees, finding that prior criminal records or personal defaults are associated
with a higher likelihood of firm-level financial distress and bankruptcy (Kallunki and Pyykkö
2013, Regenburg and Seitz 2021).

ology developed by Breiman et al. (1984). The use of this technique in the context of default
prediction is not new – Frydman, Altman, and Kao (1985) find that it outperforms discriminant

3.5. Reexamining the predictive power of accounting information
The analysis of predictive power in some earlier studies was not conducted out-of-sample, and
the sample used by Beaver et al. (2012) ends in 2002, leaving out 20 years of potentially signifi-
cant changes (e.g. Sarbanes Oxley , Dodd-Frank Act, and new accounting standards, such as ASC
842 on lease accounting). T o assess how the predictive power of accounting information has
evolved in more recent years, I estimate the probability of bankruptcy using a comprehensive
sample of U.S. Chapter 7 and Chapter 1 1 bankruptcy filings spanning from 1980 to the end of
2023. I combine bankruptcy data from two main sources: Correia et al. (2018) and Audit Ana-
lytics. Following Shumway (2001), I employ a discrete time-hazard model, incorporating three

Second, market data inherently reflects a broader set of information, including insights derived
from financial statements. This raises the important question of why accounting information has
incremental predictive power with respect to market information.
When evaluating the attributes of accounting information, it is crucial to recognise that the
design of accounting standards involves significant trade-of fs, which can introduce noise or
bias into financial reporting. These trade-offs may impair the ability of financial metrics to
fully capture key elements such as a firm’ s asset values, default barriers, and asset volatility .
However , the inherent ‘biases’ embedded in the accounting system can themselves provide valu-
able insights. In the following subsections, I examine several attributes of financial statement
data and explore their implications for bankruptcy prediction, both when used in isolation and
in conjunction with market data.
4.1. Historical cost vs. fair value
Despite the growing adoption of fair value accounting, especially under IFRS, many assets con-
tinue to be reported at modified historical cost, which can differ significantly from their current
market values. Fair value accounting often provides a closer approximation of current asset
values, potentially aiding in the assessment of a firm’ s assets – a key determinant of default
risk according to Merton (1974). However , the benefits of fair value accounting come with
trade-offs, including reduced reliability due to valuation uncertainty . Moreover , fair value
accounting primarily captures ex-post (realised) outcomes, limiting its ability to reflect ex-
ante (anticipated) risks. In contrast, historical cost accounting offers a dynamic perspective on
risk by incorporating its evaluation and resolution over time, thereby providing insights into
firm risk trajectories (Penman 2021). These distinctions highlight the complementary strengths
and limitations of fair value and historical cost regimes in bankruptcy prediction.
Moreover , while the aggregate fair value of individual assets may diff er from the fair value of
their collective use, earnings reported under historical cost accounting provide valuable insights
into the joint utilisation of assets. This includes also intangible or off-bala nce-sheet components
that may not be captured in fair value assessments. Earnings thus convey critical information
about returns, risk, and the resolution of uncertainty .
The shift toward fair value accounting may thus produce two conflicting effects in the context
of bankruptcy prediction. On the one hand, the balance sheet is likely to better reflect the market
value of a firm’ s individual assets, albeit sometimes at the expense of reduced reliability . On the

4.2. Unrecognised assets
Many assets, particularly intangible ones, are excluded from the balance sheet. W ith the rise of
the new economy , the importance of intangible assets has grown significantly over time. Invest-
ments in intangibles, such as research and development (R&D) and advertising, are primarily
expensed rather than capitalized, despite their documented association with future operating
earnings (Lev and Sougiannis 1996, Aboody and Lev 1998). One could ar gue that this practice
of expensing internally developed intangibles might diminish the utility of balance sheet figures
for assessing a firm’ s asset value. Beaver et al. (2020) challenge this idea – assets must be com-
bined to produce returns and, therefore, a firm’ s asset value may diff er from the sum of the value
of its individual assets.
Instead, information on asset values might be more ef fectively conveyed through earn-
ings, which are themselves also af fected by the decision to capitalise these assets. Penman
(2021) notes that the current requirement to expense many of these investments is not
ideal from an income statement perspective as it comingles current revenues with invest-
ments to produce future revenues. Nonetheless, a decision to capitalise would have to
carefully consider the impact of uncertainty on matching in the income statement
(Barker et al. 2022).
In thinking about the role of intangibles, and the ef fects of accounting for intangibles, on the
usefulness of accounting information for bankruptcy prediction, it seems relevant to consider
several additional factors. First, prior studies suggest that the increase in intangibles might
render market data less useful for bankruptcy prediction, in that firms with higher levels of intan-
gible investment are more likely to have mispriced equity securities (Eberhart et al. 2004, Daniel
and T itman 2006, Aksoy et al. 2008, Edmans 201 1, and Eisfeldt and Papanikolaou 2013). This
suggests a potential greater role for accounting data and careful financial statement analysis.
Second, intangible assets are associated with higher information asymmetry and higher uncer-
tainty regarding liquidation variables – they may disappear or be nontransferable as bankruptcy
approaches (W illiamson 1988, Shleifer and V ishny 1992). Third, one could argue that intangible
assets increase firm resilience (that is, their ability to cope with unexpected shocks) (e.g.
Altomonte et al. 2022), in which case, the amount of intangible assets may contain key infor-
mation for bankruptcy prediction.
Franzen, Rodgers, and Simin (2007) demonstrate that the predictive power of the O-Score
diminishes when applied to U.S. firms with high R&D intensity – where R&D expenditures
are fully expensed – compared to firms with low R&D intensity . Their findings suggest that capi-
talising R&D constructively can improve predictive performance, although this process is not
straightforward. Estimating industry amortisation rates requires careful adjustments to mitigate
survivorship biases, and estimations must be conducted out-of-sample to avoid look-ahead
biases. Notably , Franzen et al. (2007) caution that capitalisation, even if it enhances predictive
power for credit risk assessment, might also obscure income statement information related to
risk.

4.3. Absence of direct measures of historical or expected asset volatility
Another limitation is that financial statements lack direct measures of historical or expected asset
volatility . Historical volatility measures can be more easily derived from equity and credit market
prices. Likewise, expected asset volatility measures can be produced by deleveraging implied
option-market volatility .
Nevertheless, Penman (2010, 2021) ar gues that earnings can still convey valuable infor-
mation about risk and its resolution. Sridharan (2015) finds that financial statement information
can help forecast future realised equity volatility . Accordingly , Correia et al. (2018) find that the
fundamental volatility , and higher moments of accounting returns on net operating assets pro-
vides incremental predictive power for bankruptcy forecasting, even when these measures are
added to models that include forward-looking expected volatility metrics.
4.4. Off-balance sheet liabilities
Off-balance-sheet obligations, such as leases, have historically posed challenges in accu-
rately assessing a firm’ s default risk. The implementation of lease accounting standards
like IFRS 16 and ASC 842 has mandated that firms recognise most leases on their
balance sheets, thereby enhancing transparency regarding the default barrier and the level
of operating assets that the firm has in place. Correia and Urcan (2023) find that that the
adoption of these standards led to changes in the level and term structure of credit
spreads for high lease intensity firms, which are consistency with a reduction uncertainty
regarding the default barrier . However , certain commitments, notably variable leases, may
remain off- balance-sheet. Heese, Shin, and W ang (2024) find that variable-lease expenses
are common and significant, exhibiting persistence and predictability similar to operating-
lease expenses. Their study suggests that firms might substitute operating leases with vari-
able leases to maintain off- balance-sheet financing, potentially leading to an underestimation
of a firm’ s liabilities and default risk.
4.5. Discretion
The flexibility permitted by accounting standards introduces an element of managerial discretion,
rendering financial statement figures inherently subjective. The implications of this discretion are
ambiguous: while managers may exploit it opportunistically , it can also function as a signalling
mechanism to convey private information (e.g. Beaver and Engel 1996), thereby potentially
enhancing the informativeness of financial statements. The overall impact of discretion on the
informativeness of financial statements depends on the relative prevalence of signalling versus
opportunistic behaviour , which may lead to impairment, enhancement, or no significant
change in informativeness.
Managerial discretion plays a particularly important role in the context of financial distress,
as managers often have incentives to engage in earnings management and impression manage-
ment at various stages leading up to default and bankruptcy (DeFond and Jiambalvo 1994,
Beneish, Press, and V ar gus 2012; Franz, Hassab Elnaby and Lobo 2014, Dyreng et al. 2022).
These incentives can undermine the ef fectiveness of financial ratios as timely indicators of bank-
ruptcy risk. However , they also point to the potential value of non-financial predictors – particu-
larly those informed by the earnings management literature. Moreover , these dynamics highlight
how certain regulatory reforms aimed at curbing financial misreporting and enhancing disclosure
quality may , in turn, improve the predictive utility of accounting information in bankruptcy
forecasting.

4.6. Evidence from prior literature
Beaver et al. (2005) examine changes in the predictive ability of accounting and market data for
bankruptcy over the period 1962–2002. They document a slight decline in the predictive of
accounting information but find that this decline is offs et by an improvement in the incremental
predictive ability of equity market data. Beaver et al. (2012) follow up on this analysis by con-
ducting a series of cross-sectional and time-series analyses to examine how certain accounting
attributes influence the predictive performance of bankruptcy models. Specifically , they focus
on the ef fects of (i) managerial discretion, (ii) unrecognised intangible assets, (iii) the compre-
hensiveness of the accounting system, and (iv) the recognition of losses. A key challenge in
their analysis lies in disentangling the impact of accounting attributes from underlying differ -
ences in firm fundamentals.
They examine measures of discretion, including the presence of subsequent financial state-
ment restatements for a firm-year and the magnitude of discretionary accruals. They find that
the predictive power of the accounting model is significantly lower for firm-years that were
later restated. Although this reduction in predictive power also applies to the market and com-
bined models, it is less pronounced, suggesting that investors are only partially able to off set
the diminished informativeness of financial ratios using other sources of information. They
also find that the predictive accuracy of the accounting, market, and combined models is
highest for firms with moderate levels of accruals, while it diminishes for firms with
‘extreme’ accruals, whether income-increasing or income-decreasing.
T o evaluate whether unrecognised intangibles af fect the predictive power of bankruptcy
models, Beaver et al. (2012) rank firms based on their R&D intensity , measured as R&D
expenses as a percentage of sales. The results show that the predictive power of the accounting,
market, and combined models is lowest for firms with high R&D intensity . Additionally , the
market model outperforms the accounting model for these firms, supporting the interpretation
that the market partially incorporates the value of intangible assets into prices.
T o assess the comprehensiveness of the accounting system, Beaver et al. (2012) analyse
firms’ book-to-market (BTM) ratios. The behaviour of BTM ratios as firms approach bank-
ruptcy is complex – both the book value (numerator) and market value (denominator) are
expected to decline as bankruptcy probability increases, making it challenging to predict
which component will decline more rapidly . Additionally , while book value can become nega-
tive and approach zero, market value cannot, as the option value of equity persists even as the
probability of bankruptcy rises. Consequently , as losses accumulate and book value
approaches zero, the BTM ratio itself may also approach zero. T o address these complexities,
Beaver et al. (2012) partition firms into four groups based on BTM ratios: negative, low posi-
tive, medium positive, and high positive. The predictive power of the accounting, market, and
combined models is lowest for firms with negative BTM ratios (indicating negative book
value of equity) and firms with high positive BTM ratios. Conversely , predictive power is
highest for firms with low and medium positive BTM ratios. These findings suggest that pre-
dictive accuracy declines in cases where there are significant discrepancies between market
valuation and the carrying book value reported in financial statements. Furthermore, their
results indicate that investors may struggle to fully compensate for the impaired predictive
power of accounting information using alternative sources.
The final attribute investigated by Beaver et al. (2012) is the recognition of losses. Beaver
et al. (2012) find that the predictive power of the accounting model is significantly lower for
loss firm-years compared to non-loss firm-years. This finding aligns with their observation
that, conditional on the presence or absence of a loss, the coefficients on the remaining account-
ing and market variables are substantially closer to zero for loss firms. Importantly , these

disparities in predictive power persist in the market and combined models, suggesting that the
challenges posed by loss recognition are not fully mitigated by incorporating market information.
Beaver et al. (2019) leverage their international sample to examine the role of country-level
financial reporting transparency in the predictive power of accounting information. They find
that in parent companies domiciled in countries with lower financial reporting transparency –
assessed based on securities regulation, investor protection, and enforcement characteristics
supporting financial transparency – both a pure accounting model and a combined model incorpor-
ating accounting and market information exhibit reduced predictive power compared to countries
with higher financial reporting transparency . Additionally , they observe that incorporating granu-
lar subsidiary-level information in bankruptcy prediction is particularly valuable in such cases,
helping to compensate for the lower financial reporting transparency of the parent company .
Maffet, Owens, and Srinivasan (2017) provide evidence consistent with accounting infor-
mation being particularly valuable when equity market information is less informative. They
find that find that short-sale constraints decrease the predictive power of equity-market variables
for default. The incremental predictive power of accounting information is higher in this case,
especially in countries with higher-financial reporting transparency – accounting information
partially , but not fully , makes up for the lower predictive power of equity-market information.
Rather than focusing solely on the variation in the usefulness of accounting information for
predicting bankruptcy , several studies examine its relevance to credit market investors. Since
default probability is a primary determinant of credit spreads, prior research finds that credit
spreads incorporate information about forecasted default probabilities, albeit with a time lag
(Correia et al. 2012, 2018). In this spirit, Givoly , Hayn, and Katz (2017) investigate changes
in the informational content of accounting numbers for bondholders over time. Specifically ,
they examine the relationship between accounting information and bond returns. Their findings
suggest that the informational content of accounting numbers for bondholders has increased over
time. This increase is attributed to factors such as changes in credit risk, a rise in accounting con-
servatism, a shift toward fair value accounting, and a greater frequency of reported losses.

5. A venues for future r esearc h
As ESG transparency increases – driven by growing stakeholder demand and the global prolifer-
ation of ESG disclosure regulations – a relevant and underexplored question emerge s: can
ESG-related information enhance the traditional set of indicators used to predict bankruptcy?
Amiraslani et al. (2023) provide evidence that firms with high social capital experienced
lower bond spreads during the financial crisis, a period marked by elevated default risk and
diminished trust. Their findings suggest that such firms may face lower agency costs of debt
(e.g. reduced perceived risk of asset substitution or cash diversion) and benefit from stronger sta-
keholder cooperation. Future research could investigate how to efficiently summarise ESG dis-
closures, extract signals relevant to default risk, and integrate these signals with traditional
bankruptcy predictors. Understanding the relative importance of ESG dimensions in this
context remains a key area for further inquiry .
Second, and relatedly , researchers could leverage generative AI – particularly larg e
language models (LLMs) – to process the vast and heterogeneous disclosures produced by
firms (including ESG-related disclosures), with the goal of improving bankruptcy prediction
models (e.g. Kim et al. 2024, 2025). Prior literature finds that generative AI presents several
advantages vis-à-vis more traditional textual analysis techniques (Li et al. 2024, Kim et al.
2025). Moreover , LLM produced summaries may be better able to capture the sentiment of
firms’ disclosures (Kim et al. 2024), with negative sentiment being particularly relevant in
the setting of bankruptcy and default prediction. A notable challenge in this approach is the
potential for look-ahead bias (Sarkar and V afa 2024, Levy 2025), which warrants careful
methodological attention.
Third, studies could more deeply explore inter -firm linkages to assess the presence of
network ef fects and the transmission of credit risk shocks beyond traditional business group
structures (Y ang, Bir ge, and Parker 2015, W u 2024). This would involve drawing on the analysis
of complex financial networks (Elliott, Golub, and Jackson 2014). Financial disclosures can play
an important role in analysing firm linkages, a role that remains underexplored.

Fourth, the growing availability of lar ge-scale structured and unstructured data – including

6. Conclusion
Corporate failures remain a critical issue, imposing significant costs on stakeholders and fre-
quently leading to financial distress, default, and ultimately bankruptcy or liquidation. These fail-
ures have profound implications at both industry and macroeconomic levels, underscoring the
importance of accurately assessing bankruptcy risk.
This study reviews prior literature exploring the predictive power of financial statement
information, both on its own and in conjunction with market data. Understanding this predictive
power provides valuable insights into the usefulness of financial statements, a topic of consider-
able importance not only to regulators and the accounting profession but also to creditors, equity
holders and other parties concerned with forecasting financial distress.
The Merton (1974) model offers a theoretical framework for understanding how accounting
information might contribute to bankruptcy prediction. I reflect on the attributes of accounting
information that may enhance or reduce its usefulness in predicting key default primitives:
asset value, asset volatility , and the default barrier . Additionally , I review empirical studies
that investigate some of these attributes and changes in the predictive power of accounting infor-
mation over time.
I expand earlier analysis, by using a sample of bankruptcies spanning 1980–2023. This
allows me to study how the predictive power of accounting information has evolved over
the last 40 years. My findings indicate that the predictive power of accounting information
has remained relatively stable, while the predictive power of market data has increased. It is
important to note that these changes in predictive power may reflect, in addition to changes
in accounting standards, economic conditions, or firm composition. Nevertheless, this analysis
highlights the enduring value of accounting information, which has consistently demonstrated
strong predictive utility and often compensates for declines in the predictive power of equity
market data.
The paper concludes with the discussion of several potential avenues for future research.
